# What I Learned in My First Engineering Entrepreneurship Competition

I used to think the best engineering solution was the most efficient one.

As engineering students, we're often trained to think this way. We learn to maximize efficiency, minimize losses, and improve performance wherever possible. That mindset was challenged when I competed in an engineering entrepreneurship competition.

My team developed a hydropower concept that harvested energy from pressure reducing valves (PRVs) in water distribution systems. At first glance, our goal seemed straightforward: maximize the amount of energy we could generate.

We began mapping areas with high elevation gradients and calculating the potential energy available within existing water infrastructure. But as we researched existing solutions and prepared for the competition, we realized that technical performance was only part of the problem.

A highly efficient system deployed at a handful of locations might generate more power per installation, but wasn't that already being done? Wasn't that exactly what existing hydropower companies were focused on?

What made our idea different when industry professionals had already spent years researching this concept and pushing efficiency to its limits?

That was the problem: we weren't different. We were trying to improve upon a path that experts had already explored extensively.

So we asked ourselves a different question:

**What could we do that others weren't doing?**

Instead of competing to build the most efficient system, we decided to rethink who the technology was designed for.

Rather than targeting only the highest-pressure sites where energy generation could be maximized, we explored the possibility of a lower-cost, more standardized design that could be deployed across a much larger number of locations. While each installation might generate less power, the broader accessibility and scalability of the system could create greater overall impact.

The question shifted from:

**"How do we maximize efficiency?"**

to

**"How do we maximize impact?"**

That change in perspective became the most valuable lesson I took away from the competition.

I learned that engineering is not always about creating the most technically optimized solution. Sometimes the most successful innovation comes from understanding the problem differently and designing a solution that people can realistically adopt and implement.

The competition introduced me to a way of thinking that I had not encountered as often in my engineering courses: customer-centered design.

When engineers approach a problem, it is tempting to focus solely on technical specifications. We ask questions like:

* How efficient is the system?
* How can we improve performance?

While these questions are important, entrepreneurship encouraged me to ask a different set of questions:

* Who will use this technology?
* What challenges do they face?

As we refined our project, we began considering the needs of water utilities and municipalities.

Even if a highly efficient system existed, would it be affordable to install? Would it require extensive customization? Would the return justify the investment?

These considerations forced us to think beyond the engineering itself and consider the practical realities of deployment.

Technical excellence is still valuable, but only if it contributes to a solution that can be realistically implemented. A technology that achieves slightly lower performance but can be deployed at hundreds of locations may ultimately have a greater impact than a highly optimized solution that is only feasible in a select few cases.

Looking back, I realize that the competition was not really about the technology. It was about learning how innovation happens.

Innovation is not always the result of inventing something entirely new. Sometimes it comes from identifying a gap that others have overlooked and approaching a familiar problem from a different perspective.

This experience has changed how I approach engineering projects today. I now try to think more broadly about the people, constraints, and systems surrounding a design.

Instead of asking only whether a solution works, I ask whether it can realistically be adopted and create value in the real world.

That mindset has become one of the most valuable lessons I have carried forward from the competition.
