# Why Engineering Students Should Learn Entrepreneurship
Most engineers will build their careers in established companies, government agencies, research institutions, or consulting firms, and there is absolutely nothing wrong with that. Not every engineering student needs to become a founder, nor should that be the expectation.

While entrepreneurship is often associated with launching startups, its value extends far beyond company creation. Regardless of career path, I believe engineering students can benefit from learning entrepreneurial thinking.

I thought my job was to focus on technical problems while someone else worried about customers, markets, and business models. If an engineer designed a solution and it worked, then the job was done.

Over time, I realized that many of the most important challenges facing engineers are not purely technical. A solution can be innovative, efficient, and technically sound, yet still fail to make an impact if it doesn’t adopt well. Understanding why people choose to use a technology, how organizations make decisions, and what constraints exist beyond the design itself is often just as important as the engineering.

I didn't learn entrepreneurship because I wanted to start a company. I learned it because it made me a better engineer.

Entrepreneurship taught me to think about problems from a different perspective. Instead of focusing exclusively on performance and optimization, I began asking broader questions. Who will use this solution? What challenges do they face? What would prevent them from adopting it? How can a design create value in a way that is practical and scalable?

These are not business questions. They are engineering questions. They simply approach engineering from the perspective of impact rather than technology alone.
## Think Beyond the Design
One of the most valuable lessons entrepreneurship taught me was the importance of customer-centered design.

In many engineering courses, success is often measured by technical performance. The better design is the one that is more efficient, more accurate, or more optimized. While these metrics are important, they only tell part of the story.

I first realized this while competing in an engineering entrepreneurship competition. My team was developing a hydropower concept that harvested energy from pressure reducing valves (PRVs) in water distribution systems. Initially, our focus was on maximizing energy generation. We mapped areas with high elevation gradients, calculated potential energy output, and researched how to improve performance.

As we researched existing solutions, however, we encountered an important question: What made our approach different?

Existing hydropower companies had already spent years optimizing their technologies and targeting locations where energy generation could be maximized. If our goal was simply to create a more efficient system, we were competing against organizations with far more experience and resources than we had.

Instead of asking how we could generate the most energy, we began asking who would actually use our technology and what challenges they faced. We considered the needs of water utilities and municipalities. Would the system be affordable to install? Would it require extensive customization? Would the return on investment justify the cost?

In engineering courses, projects often end once the design is complete. If the calculations are correct and the solution meets the requirements, the project is considered a success. In the real world, however, implementation can be just as challenging as the design itself.

A technically sound solution may still face budget constraints, regulatory hurdles, maintenance concerns, or resistance to change. Engineers rarely work in isolation; their designs exist within larger systems involving stakeholders, organizations, and competing priorities.

Learning entrepreneurship helped me appreciate these realities. It encouraged me to think not only about whether a solution could be built, but also whether it could realistically be implemented. In many cases, the difference between a successful project and an unsuccessful one is not the quality of the technology itself, but the ability to navigate the constraints surrounding it.

## Engineering for Impact
These questions ultimately changed the direction of our project. Rather than pursuing the most efficient design possible, we explored a lower-cost, more standardized approach that could potentially be deployed at a larger number of sites. The experience taught me that a technically superior solution is not always the most impactful one. Sometimes the best solution is the one that balances performance with practicality and accessibility.

Although it was an engineering competition, much of the judging focused on entrepreneurship. The technical aspects of the project were important, but they were only one piece of the puzzle. The judges wanted to know who would use the technology, why they would adopt it, and how it would create value in the real world.

That experience changed the way I think about engineering. A great idea alone is not enough. Engineers must also consider how their solutions are communicated and implemented. The most innovative technology in the world will have little impact if nobody uses it.

Entrepreneurship did not replace engineering thinking; it expanded it. It encouraged me to think not only about whether a solution could work, but also whether it would reach the people it was designed to help.
